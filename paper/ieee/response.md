# Reviewer 1

## Reviewer 1, Concern 1: Unproven validity condition

> Your headline results are certified using the Maurer–Pontil bound, which Theorem 1 states is proven only for independent draws; validity under the without-replacement sampling your procedure actually performs is explicitly not proven. You stress-test this empirically (Section V.l, V.k) and find no violations, which is reassuring, but the paper would be considerably stronger if the primary reported numbers used the proven Bardenet–Maillard bound, with Maurer–Pontil results presented as a secondary, empirically-validated variant rather than the headline procedure.

**Author response:** We agree that the headline numbers must rest on a bound proven for the sampling actually performed, and we went one step further than the suggestion. Reviewer 3 (Concern 2) asked why an exact bound was not used. On reflection the exact bound is the right choice, not merely a comparison point. The flip indicator is binary, and the sample is drawn uniformly without replacement from a frozen stratum. Conditional on the stratum's fresh-draw outcomes, the sampled flip count is therefore exactly hypergeometric. The finite-population Clopper–Pearson construction then gives an upper confidence bound that is valid for every population realization, hence unconditionally. It bounds the realized whole-stratum flip count, the quantity the estimand is defined on, where the earlier theorem bounded its expectation. It is also tighter than either empirical-Bernstein bound, because a binary loss never needs their range term. On a clean sample of 90 draws at the pinned per-look level, the exact bound reads {{m:boundExactAtN}} where Maurer–Pontil reads {{m:boundEbAtN}} and Bardenet–Maillard reads {{m:boundWorAtN}}. Bardenet–Maillard is proven for this sampling, but adopting it as the headline would have cost power for no gain in validity. It certifies the same {{m:boundWorConfigs}} configurations as Maurer–Pontil, 3 of them at the same look and 4 at a later one.

One clause of the comment needs a correction on our side. The first version reported that its stress tests found no violations, and the reviewer took reassurance from that. Under an exact bound the statement cannot stand, because an exact test spends its failure probability instead of leaving it unused. The revision reports unsafe certificates as a rate: at most {{m:calUnsafeWorst}} of planted runs in any configuration, and at most {{m:calBoundaryHi}} at the least favorable population, against a nominal {{m:calNominalDelta}} per stratum. Item 3 of the list at the end of this document gives the detail.

**Author action:** The pinned certifier now uses the exact finite-population bound (Section {{ref:sec:system}}, "The bound", Eq. {{ref:eq:exact}}; Algorithm {{ref:alg:sivm}}). Theorem {{ref:thm:valid}} is restated for that bound and proven in full by the coupling argument. The theorem's last sentence and a remark after the proof state that the same argument covers any bound valid for uniform without-replacement draws, including Bardenet–Maillard, while Maurer–Pontil is retained only as an ablation arm. Every result in the manuscript (Table {{ref:tab:main}}, the calibration study, the baselines, the SUPG-style competitor, the tables for the other models, the deployment section) was regenerated under the exact bound from the same stored labels and oracle draws. The bound ablation (Section {{ref:sec:exp-ablations}}, "The bound ablation"; Table {{ref:tab:bounds}}; Appendix {{ref:app:grid}}) now compares five bounds inside the same procedure: exact, Clopper–Pearson (binomial), betting sequence, Maurer–Pontil, and Bardenet–Maillard, with their null-calibration results. The Availability section names the public preprint of the first version and states that its certification results are reproducible from the same release with the Maurer–Pontil arm selected, so nothing about the earlier version is lost.

## Reviewer 1, Concern 3: Equivalence relation scope

> Exact-match equivalence limits applicability to Boolean/select columns. Since your introduction motivates free-text "spreadsheet copilot" columns, even a small pilot with a judge-based equivalence relation (with its own error calibrated into $\alpha$, as you note in Section VIII) would substantially broaden the paper's relevance.

**Author response:** We ran the pilot the reviewer describes, and its result is negative on power. The column is a free-text one over the same Stack Overflow profiles (the respondent's primary technical specialty in at most eight words). It has three prompt versions (a baseline, a formatting-only edit of it, and a synonym rewording), n=2,000 rows, and one fresh draw per cell from the primary model. The equivalence relation is a judge: the primary model, asked in both orders whether two phrases name the same specialty, with agreement required in both orders. The calibration recipe of Section {{ref:sec:limits}} was applied as stated. The author hand-labeled {{m:pilotLabeled}} pairs from the judge's output ({{m:pilotLabeledSame}} it had called equivalent and {{m:pilotLabeledDiff}} it had called different, drawn evenly from the three comparisons and pooled). A second model (meta/muse-spark-1.3-contributor) labeled the same pairs under the same instruction as an independent check.

The two labelers read the judge differently, and the manuscript reports both readings.

- *Under the author's labels* the judge missed nothing: of the {{m:pilotLabeledSame}} pairs it had called equivalent, the author called {{m:pilotMisses}} different. That gives an upper confidence bound of {{m:pilotMissUcb}} on its miss rate among the pairs it calls equivalent at $\delta_{\mathrm{cal}}=0.05$ (pooled across the three comparisons; each comparison's {{m:pilotLabeledSamePerComparison}} labels alone give {{m:pilotMissUcbPerComparison}}). The recipe takes that bound off each budget, {{m:pilotMissUcbPts}} percentage points, so $\alpha=0.2$ becomes {{m:pilotAlphaLooseEff}} and $\alpha=0.1$ becomes {{m:pilotAlphaTightEff}}.
- *Under the second labeler's labels* the judge missed {{m:pilotAdjMissesOnJudgeSame}} of those {{m:pilotLabeledSame}} pairs, all of them pairs the author called the same. The miss bound would then be {{m:pilotAdjMissUcb}}, a deflation of {{m:pilotAdjMissUcbPts}} points, more than the tight budget itself. The clean side of the calibration therefore rests on the author's reading, and the manuscript says so.
- *On the other side* the author called 41 of the judge's {{m:pilotLabeledDiff}} "different" pairs the same specialty, while the second labeler called 42 of the {{m:pilotLabeledDiff}} different. By the author's labels the judge is stricter than the author's reading; by the second labeler's it is mostly right. One human labeler and one model cannot say which reading is right. A second human labeler is what would separate a strict instruction from a lenient labeler, and the manuscript says that as well.

Under the judge, the column's own floor (two draws of the same prompt) is {{m:pilotFloorRate}}, the formatting edit's flip rate {{m:pilotFmtRate}}, and the synonym edit's {{m:pilotSynRate}} (over 1,602, 1,821, and 1,799 usable pairs). The certifier therefore refused every stratum of both edits at both budgets at its first looks. Weighting the judge's verdicts by the author's labels puts the disagreement of two same-prompt draws near 7.9% (band [4.4, 14.5]%), inside the loose budget. The second model agreed with the hand labels on 74.1% of pairs, and the judge agreed with them on 79.6%.

The pilot does not broaden the savings claims, and the manuscript does not present it as doing so. It shows three things. The machinery ports unchanged to a judge-based equivalence. The judge's error enters the budget exactly as the recipe says, at a price that depends on whose labels calibrate it. The larger price of a judge is power: the certifier sees only the judge, and it refused. The composed guarantee holds for any judge whose miss rate the calibration bounds correctly; what the pilot does not deliver is a judge whose equivalence matches a reader's, calibrated in both directions.

**Author action:** Section {{ref:sec:limits}}, "Equivalence and columns", states the recipe with its $1-\delta-\delta_{\mathrm{cal}}$ accounting and the homogeneity assumption it rests on. ## Reviewer 2, Concern 1: Clearer explanation of the certification framework

> The authors should provide a clearer explanation of the proposed certification and verification framework, including its assumptions, workflow, correctness criteria, and how it determines whether a previously computed cell can be safely reused after a prompt change.

**Author response:** We agree that the first version asked the reader to assemble the framework from several places. In one paragraph, the framework is this. An AI column is a view (a prompt, a model, and pinned decode parameters), and each cached cell is one draw from that model. After a prompt edit the question is not whether a cached cell is correct. It is whether recomputing the cell under the new prompt would return a different value (a flip). Learning that for one cell costs exactly the call that reuse would save, so sIVM never decides per cell. It (1) diffs the two prompts, for provenance only; (2) partitions the cached cells into strata fixed before any model call, by cached value; (3) recomputes a seeded sample from each stratum on a doubling schedule of looks; (4) at each look computes an exact finite-population upper confidence bound on the stratum's flip rate, certifies the stratum when that bound clears the user's error budget $\alpha$, and refuses it when the sample's own flip rate already exceeds $\alpha$; (5) reuses every unsampled cell of a certified stratum and recomputes every cell of a refused one.

The correctness criterion is Theorem {{ref:thm:valid}}. With probability at least $1-\delta$ over a run's sampling and oracle draws, the run issues no wrong certificate: none for a stratum in which more than an $\alpha$ share of the cells would flip if recomputed now. Three assumptions, stated together in Section {{ref:sec:problem}}, carry it: the model snapshot is pinned and draws do not interfere across rows (Assumption {{ref:as:pin}}), the stratification is fixed before sampling (Assumption {{ref:as:frozen}}), and the sample is a uniform without-replacement draw within each stratum (Assumption {{ref:as:sample}}). The guarantee is per stratum and about the realized run. It is a statement about the procedure, not the probability that a given certificate is right, and it says nothing about agreement with ground truth. For an unedited prompt a budget below the model's own rate of disagreement with itself is rarely certifiable, the impossibility floor of Section {{ref:sec:problem}}. The revision states each of these elements explicitly, at the places listed below.

8. **Three operating rules, stated and enforced.** Section {{ref:sec:impl}} now states three rules of live operation. A sampled cell whose oracle call fails is counted as a flip and left for recomputation; the released certifier had dropped such cells, and it now counts them. The rule is conservative, since a failure can only add to the count the bound sees. No certification reported in the article is affected, because the benchmark replications and the deployment replays run on stored, completed draws; the one study where the convention matters is the independence check, which now reports both (Reviewer 3, Concern 5). Reuse does not chain: a reused cell is a copy of the previous version's value, the certifier takes only computed cells as its cache, and a reused cell is therefore recomputed at the next edit. Section {{ref:sec:limits}} has a new paragraph, "Sequences of edits", that states the consequence (savings are per edit) and says that the theorem itself would allow a later certification of the carried-forward values, which we have not evaluated. And the sampling seed of a certification defaults to a hash of the column and its two versions (the released certifier had defaulted to one fixed seed), so successive certifications of a column draw different samples; the experiments set their seeds explicitly, as before.

9. **How a certificate is to be read.** Section {{ref:sec:problem}}, "Reading the budget", now states the guarantee as what it is, a bound on the share of runs that issue a wrong certificate, and says that it is not the probability that a certificate in hand is right. Section {{ref:sec:system}} lists what is fixed before the first oracle call, and marks the two results that were computed after their draws had been seen (the replay of the August draws under the exact bound, and the graded re-score) as retrospective where they appear.

10. **The released certify action now runs the pinned procedure.** Reading the released code against the manuscript's description, we found that the table's certify action called the certifier in an earlier configuration: one fixed-size look per stratum, and strata refined by the lexical overlap of the edit's terms. That variant is valid (a single look is a special case of the schedule, and any frozen stratifier preserves validity), but it is not the procedure the article evaluates. The action now runs the pinned procedure (cached-value strata, the exact bound, the doubling schedule with the futility rule), and the generator checks that it does. Section {{ref:sec:impl}} states the action's scope: the rows of the user's current view, at most {{m:routeScopeCap}} per request, with the corpus-scale runs calling the same certifier function from a script. Each request is a certification of its own, in the default mode. A stored certificate now also names, for each stratum, the level, the mode, and the bound it was tested under, so that its decision can be re-derived from the record alone; the manuscript notes that the certificates of Section {{ref:sec:exp-deploy}} predate that field and carry those settings in their run artifacts.
